## A Cat, a Projector, and a Wall

A few years ago, we bought our first projector.

Naturally, the first thing I did was set it up in the kitchen, point it at the nearest wall, and start watching YouTube.

One of the videos featured dogs dancing. Hunter, my cat, loves playing with dogs in the park. So, when he saw a dog on the screen, he immediately started jumping toward it.

Unfortunately, there was nothing there but a wall.

Hunter could see the dog. He could hear the dog. But it turned out that it wasn't a dog at all.

Let's take a closer look at this example and examine what Hunter actually perceived, what he already knew, and what decisions he made.

## What Does It Mean to See a Dog?

What does it actually mean when we say that Hunter sees a dog?

During the day, in a park, sunlight travels through space and reaches the dog. Photons interact with specific atoms that make up the dog's body. Some of the resulting reflected or scattered photons travel in different directions, with different energies and wavelengths. Some eventually reach Hunter's eyes, where the retina converts the incoming light into electrical signals that travel to his brain.

In the evening, in our kitchen, something remarkably similar happens. The difference is that the source of the photons is the projector's lamp. The light passes through the projector's imaging system, which produces an image by directing light of different intensities and colors toward the wall. The light reflects off the wall and enters Hunter's eyes, where the retina once again converts it into electrical signals sent to his brain.

From the brain's perspective, the incoming signals are remarkably similar to those produced when Hunter sees a real dog in the park.

Hunter has seen many dogs in different conditions and environments, in different parks, during the day and at night. Through these experiences, his brain has developed an internal representation of what a dog looks like.

When he sees the image on the wall, it matches that representation closely enough for him to recognize a dog.

But it is only an image on a wall.

Hunter has also heard many dogs during their encounters in the park. They behave differently: some approach silently to make friends, some bark, and others hide behind their owners. But Hunter has a pretty good idea of what a dog sounds like.

So, when he hears familiar sounds coming from the projector's speakers, he concludes that a dog is barking.

There is another way to recognize a dog: smell. In the real world, in the park, Hunter can smell the dogs he encounters. In the kitchen, however, there is no dog smell.

Yet Hunter initially decides that seeing a dog and hearing a dog means that a real dog is right there in front of him—even without the familiar smell.

## Information and Data

In both cases—in the park and in the kitchen—Hunter receives information. This information is carried by physical particles of our universe that travel along a path before reaching Hunter's retina and being converted into electrical signals.

Information exists in our universe just as ordinary physical particles do. It can arise naturally and be converted into electrical signals inside a living organism or a sensor. It can also be artificially generated as electrical signals and stored somewhere within our networks.

In the kitchen, there is also data. The projector receives a structured stream of bits in a format used by YouTube and converts it into an image displayed on the wall.

Data is a structured sequence of physical states representing signals, stored in some physical medium or device.

Can data exist without information?

Yes, of course. We can take a data structure and randomly generate values that conform to that structure. Is it data? Certainly. It has the required structure.

Is it information? Not necessarily. If the generated values have no correlation with anything they are supposed to describe, they carry no information about that subject.

There is a relationship between the world, its elements, and the information we receive about them. This correlation allows our brains to build a model of the world and of the things that exist within it.

Without such a correlation, we may have data, but we have no information about the world.

## Knowledge

Knowledge can be represented in the form of information or data. Like everything else, it must be represented by physical elements of our universe.

But knowledge is not simply a record of something that has some connection to real-world processes. Knowledge is something we have tested through repeated interactions with the real world.

Hunter's experience illustrates this particularly well: even very similar information can lead to different conclusions depending on the context and the knowledge used to interpret it.

When Hunter sees a dog in the park, he understands that it is a living animal he can play with. When he sees an image of a dog projected onto the kitchen wall, however, he understands that it is just an image.

In fact, Hunter often comes to watch these projections. He watches dogs, birds, and even enjoys various movies. But he has learned that these images are not real animals. There is no point in trying to play with them or hunt them.

Hunter has expanded his knowledge by adding another model: the model of an image projected onto a wall. He can now distinguish between a real animal and its representation, even when both produce very similar visual and auditory signals.

## The OCA Loop: From Information to Knowledge

The OCA loop is the key mechanism that transforms information into knowledge.

OCA stands for Observation, Computation, and Action.

By receiving information directly from the world, evaluating and analyzing it, making decisions, and taking action, we can test our understanding against reality. Repeating this process many times—dozens, thousands, or even millions of times—allows us to turn information into knowledge.

Each cycle gives us an opportunity to compare what we expected with what actually happened. When reality does not match our expectations, we can adjust our internal model and make better decisions in the future.

Hunter's experience is a simple example of this process. He encountered real dogs, learned how they looked and sounded, and developed expectations about how to interact with them. When he encountered a projected image, his initial interpretation was incorrect. Through experience, he learned to distinguish images from real animals.

This knowledge enables us to understand the world around us, build a model of reality, and act effectively within it.

Information gives us signals about the world. Data provides a structured representation of signals. Knowledge is a tested model of the world that helps us interpret information and decide what to do.

And the OCA loop is what allows that model to evolve through interaction with reality.
