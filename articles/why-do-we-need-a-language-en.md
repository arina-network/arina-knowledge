# Why do we need a Language?
_Author [Slava Zemlianskyi](https://www.linkedin.com/in/zemlianskyi/), published 2026-11-21 in [Arina Knowledge](https://github.com/arina-network/arina-knowledge)_

_Do cats actually talk? And what about apes?

When Hunter, my cat, and I wake up, we usually head to the kitchen.
He jumps onto the table, I sit down and pet him while Hunter finishes his last dream.
Sometimes I wake up early, and Hunter simply rolls over to the other side of his chair, while sometimes I want to sleep a little longer, and then Hunter jumps onto my bed and starts stomping on me.

We don't talk about any of this.

We have done it hundreds of times and know each other's habits well enough that each of us can simply look at the other and decide what to do.
We coordinate our actions based on what we observe, without having to explain anything or exchange a single word.
Or, if we put it in terms of communication theory, we synchronize our internal semantic models.

But, like other Chausie cats, Hunter is not particularly silent. 
With a wild jungle cat and an Abyssinian in his ancestry, a Chausie doesn't just meow — he talks.
Hunter has an entire spectrum of sounds in his repertoire, from melodic chirps and trills to low-frequency growls, which he uses to remind me about dinner, ask what is going on, or demand that I play with him.

During our walks, Hunter often talks to dogs when approaching them.
Of course, most dogs have absolutely no idea what is going on or what this large, calm predator wants from them, and quite a few end up hiding behind their owners.
He also likes to negotiate with crows sitting on tree branches.
After a couple of incidents in which Hunter jumped almost two meters straight up at an overly arrogant crow, they stopped taking the risk of coming down, while Hunter turned out to be smart enough not to chase them up the trees.

Hunter also knows quite a few commands, from “no” to “wait” and “down”, although he follows them only when he understands why.
Definitely not for treats — treats are useful for negotiating photo sessions or other activities that make little sense to him.
Sometimes we simply talk, for example, when it is time to leave a comfortable sofa by the panoramic window in a café and continue our walk.
Hunter may spend some time emotionally explaining that he is perfectly happy here and has absolutely no intention of going anywhere, which often attracts the attention of everyone around us.

Many living creatures can coordinate their actions without language.
When fish swim in a school, they sense the movements of other fish through changes in water pressure and adjust their own movements accordingly, while a mother tiger teaching her cubs to hunt gradually demonstrates every stage of the process, step by step, starting with the simplest and progressing to the most difficult techniques.
Each of these creatures has its own model of the world, but it can modify that model by observing other living creatures and adjusting its behavior accordingly.
Humans do the same, although we most often use language to transmit information because it is simply faster and easier, making the coordination of our actions considerably more efficient.

Language appeared long before humans.
Birds, for example, use different sounds to distinguish between types of threats and respond accordingly, hiding from a hawk or taking off when a ground predator approaches.
Japanese tits can arrange sounds into specific sequences in which order matters, much like words in a sentence, while African drongos have even learned to lie by imitating the alarm calls of other animals to make them flee and abandon their food.

Among social predators that hunt in groups, communication becomes even more flexible, evolving into a tool for complex tactics and social interaction.

Wolves combine acoustic signals — howling, growling, and whining — with elaborate body language involving the position of their ears and tails.
Pack howling is not merely an expression of emotion but a way to locate one another over long distances, mark territory, and coordinate movements when surrounding prey.

Dolphins have taken things even further, developing one of the most complex communication systems in nature. Each dolphin invents its own unique signature whistle during childhood, which functions much like a name, allowing other dolphins to call it by imitating that sound. Dolphins have regional dialects, pass knowledge from parents to offspring, and follow conversational rules: they listen without interrupting and take turns responding.

In the wild, chimpanzees, bonobos, and gorillas use dozens of deliberate gestures and vocalizations, although their vocal anatomy is not well suited to producing complex speech.
When scientists removed this physical barrier by teaching them sign language or symbol-based communication using keyboards, their actual potential became much more apparent.

Gorilla Koko learned more than a thousand signs and reportedly understood around two thousand English words, while also being able to joke and describe her emotions.
Bonobo Kanzi learned to combine symbols on a special board, forming simple expressions such as “want banana” or “dog bite”.

Most languages in nature are acoustic, which makes sense because sound travels through air and water in all directions, goes around obstacles, and can be detected relatively easily through different sensory mechanisms.
Vision, of course, conveys much more information, but it requires light, complex sensory systems, line of sight, and continuous observation, which is not particularly convenient when a creature is hunting prey — or hiding from a predator.
A group that can coordinate its actions finds food more efficiently and is less likely to die from various dangers.

Language emerges within groups of living creatures, and each creature learns it by observing others.
Hunter is a good example: he knows enough dogs to understand them, while a dog seeing him for the first time has absolutely no idea what Hunter wants.
But let them play together for a while, and the dog starts recognizing the difference between an invitation to play and a warning to stop.

Our conversations with Hunter are very simple — they are only about actions here and now.
I cannot explain to him where exactly we are going for a walk today, why we go to the supermarket to buy meat in the evening instead of hunting ducks in the reeds. 
What an elevator, a car, or an air conditioner is, or why he cannot hunt the mouse running across the screen of my tablet.

Language allows a group of living creatures, even as different as a human and a cat, to coordinate their actions and synchronize their knowledge of the surrounding world.
Primitive acoustic signals are enough for simple coordination of the “fight or poke” kind.
But coordinating actions across complex, long-running processes requires exchanging much more information, much faster and more precisely.
Languages evolve along with the increasing complexity of semantic models of the surrounding world.
They become more complex in structure and create their own virtual worlds, which themselves require semantic models to be built in order to be understood and used effectively.
