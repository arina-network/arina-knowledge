## a Cat and a Food

My cat, Hunter, loves hunting. 
When he hears a sound in the bushes or reeds, he freezes and tries to figure out what it is. 
It could be a bird, a mouse, a snake, or something else entirely.

Sometimes he reacts immediately, jumping straight at whatever has caught his attention.
But more often, he stops and carefully tries to determine what made the sound and where exactly it is coming from.
Then he begins to approach. 
Slowly and cautiously, placing each paw with extraordinary precision, making absolutely no noise. 
He can remain in this state for quite some time — perhaps a minute, perhaps ten minutes, or even half an hour.
And then, at just the right moment, he leaps and catches his prey.

Hunter does not kill or eat what he catches.
He lives at home, where people provide him with breakfast and dinner on a regular schedule. 
But in the wild, hunting is not entertainment for cats.
It is how they obtain food.
They hunt because they need energy to survive.

## Cats always spend Energy

When hunting, Hunter relies on his knowledge of the world.
He knows how to move through bushes without making noise, what birds and rodents look and sound like, and how to position himself and jump to catch them.
He also spends a considerable amount of time figuring out what is where and how best to approach his prey.

But why spend so much time doing this? Why not simply jump immediately?

Because observation and preparation, although they also consume time and energy, are necessary to make the hunt successful.
A jump is an energy-intensive action.
Moreover, if Hunter simply jumps at random, roughly in the direction of the sound, he might not land in the right place to catch his prey and the prey would escape.

For Hunter, this would be a minor disappointment.
For a wild cat, however, it could mean losing its next meal.

## the Mouse's perspective

From the mouse's perspective, the situation is quite different.
Inside its burrow, a mouse is relatively safe.
But there is no food there. 
If it never leaves, it will eventually die of hunger. So it has no choice but to venture into the dangerous outside world and search for food.
Yet every movement can produce a sound that a predator might hear, leading to the mouse being caught and eaten.

The mouse must therefore continuously analyze its surroundings.
It needs to understand where food might be found, whether it can reach it safely, and whether there is an immediate threat from a predator.
When it hears an unusual sound or notices some movement, it freezes, just like Hunter does. 
It tries to understand what is happening, where the potential threat is coming from, and which direction it should move to hide or escape.

The mouse learns to distinguish these things through its own experience. 
It gradually learns which sounds are simply part of the surrounding environment, which indicate no immediate danger, and which signal the presence of a predator that might catch and eat it.

Some mice are better at making these distinctions than others. 
Those that do it well are more likely to survive and reproduce. 
Those that do not are eventually likely to become prey for a wild cat.

## Can a Mouse survive without its sight and hearing?

Imagine an experiment: what would happen if a mouse lost both its sight and hearing?
Would it still be able to survive?

If we placed such a mouse in a laboratory with a safe environment, where food and water were always available in predictable locations, it would most likely survive.
It could move around, find food and water, and retreat to its burrow.
The task would be relatively simple because the environment is controlled and predictable.

In the wild, without predators, the task would be more difficult.
The mouse would not be able to see obstacles, identify the best routes, or detect changes in its surroundings visually or acoustically.
Nevertheless, in many cases, it could still find its way to food, water, and a safe hiding place.

But predators change everything.
A mouse that cannot see or hear approaching predators becomes easy prey.
Its chances of survival drop dramatically.
Consequently, a population of such mice would quickly disappear in an environment with predators, simply because they would be eaten.

Even simple organisms, such as bacteria, use light or chemical signals to sense their surroundings and choose actions that help them obtain nutrients or avoid danger.
If deprived of the ability to sense the external environment, some organisms might still survive under certain conditions.
But in most natural environments, they would quickly perish.
It is this ability to perceive and interpret the surrounding world that allows organisms to maintain their safety, find food, and avoid being eaten.

In an imaginary world, every action performed by a living organism might lead to obtaining food. In the real world, however, most actions that are not based on knowledge result in energy loss or, in some cases, death.

## What does a Mouse know about itself?

In addition to knowledge about the external world, a mouse also needs knowledge about its own body.

If the mouse has already eaten enough, there is no reason to risk leaving its burrow to search for more food.
Additional food would not provide any meaningful benefit if its body does not need it.

On the other hand, if the mouse has not eaten for a long time, it must leave its burrow and search for food, even if predators are nearby. 
Otherwise, it will simply starve to death inside its supposedly safe shelter.

Therefore, choosing the right action requires not only understanding the external environment but also understanding the current state and needs of one's own body.

## the Cost of Knowledge

Acquiring knowledge about the external world requires energy. 
A living organism must spend energy to perceive its surroundings, process the incoming information, transform it into an internal representation, and incorporate it into its existing knowledge.
However, this is usually much less expensive than experimenting with the external world blindly, without any information or prior knowledge about it.
Such trial and error can consume far more energy and, in some cases, cost the organism its life.

Information consists of particles arriving from the external world and interacting with a living organism.
Inside the organism, these interactions trigger transformations that produce other particles, which form the organism's internal knowledge.
Every operation involving these particles requires energy: receiving information, processing it, transforming it into knowledge, and transmitting commands that initiate actions.

However, the total energy cost of this entire knowledge process is significantly lower than the cost of acting blindly in the external environment, without information and having no knowledge of it.

Knowledge is a tool developed through evolution to dramatically increase a live creature's chances of survival in a complex external world.
The world is full of processes, but only some of them are beneficial to a living organism.
Many others are irrelevant, dangerous, or even fatal.

Knowledge allows a living creature to distinguish between these processes, identify opportunities, recognize threats, and choose actions that improve its chances of survival with relatively low cost, gathering enough energy.
