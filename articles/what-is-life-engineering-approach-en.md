# What Is Life? An Engineering Approach
_Author [Slava Zemlianskyi](https://www.linkedin.com/in/zemlianskyi/), published 2026-10-12 in [Arina Knowledge](https://github.com/arina-network/arina-knowledge)_


Philosophers have debated the question of what life is since the days of Ancient Greece. There are many definitions of life, used by different people in different contexts. But I propose looking at this question from an engineering perspective.

The goal is not to establish an ultimate definition of life, but to develop a practical model that allows us to describe live creatures, understand how they operate, identify the conditions required for their existence, and use this knowledge to design and build more effective solutions.

I have a cat, Hunter, he's a Chausie F2. He cannot reproduce because of the genetic characteristics of his breed. Chausies are hybrids, and males of certain filial generations, from F2 to F4, usually is infertile. Nevertheless, he is alive. He runs, hunts, sleeps, and asks questions. Chausies like to talk in own language. So reproduction is not a necessary condition for an creature to be alive.

Let's take a closer look at what Hunter actually does.

He gathers information. He listens, looks, smells, and sometimes simply touches things with his paw or whiskers. He interprets what is happening around him and makes decisions based on that information and his own desires. Should he play, hunt, or sleep? He acts on his decisions and, in doing so, shapes his own life.

We can used a classic **OODA loop** (Observation-Orientation-Decision-Action) to structure his activtities. Originally developed as a decision-making model, it can be applied to virtually any living organism—or, more broadly, to any system that makes decisions. From this perspective, a key condition for life is the ability to obtain information about the surrounding world, process that information, make decisions, and act on them.

We can simplify this classic cycle into what we call the **OCA loop**: Observation-Computation-Action. This formulation allows us to extend the model to simpler organisms and information systems that have even the most primitive information-processing mechanisms. Consider an organism that responds to nothing more than the presence or absence of light. It detects a condition, processes that information in some way, and produces some actions. Even this minimal cycle fits within the OCA loop.

Computers do essentially the same thing. They receive information, process it, and produce outputs that control various automated devices. Yet we do not consider computers to be alive. So what is the difference between a cat and a computer?

First of all, cat requires **Energy** to stay alive. Every day, Hunter comes to me and tells me it is time to feed him. In his case, that means breakfast or dinner. He doesn't have to hunt but still need food, which he recieves from people. In the wild, cats are hunters. They catch prey and eat them. If they repeatedly fail to catch anything, they will eventually starve to death. It is harsh, but that is how biological life works.

Energy, therefore, is another essential condition for life. But it is not just energy in the abstract. What matters is the ability to obtain the energy required to sustain one's existence. Without energy, this existence will eventually end. A living system must do more than consume energy. Its continued existence depends on a relationship between its actions and its access to the resources it needs.

Hunter does not exist in an abstract vacuum, he exists somewhere: he sleeps in an apartment, he walks in several parks, and he travels inside a car with me. These are the places where his life unfolds. **Space**, therefore, is another essential condition for life. A living organism needs not only information and energy, but also a space in which to exist and interact with its surroundings.

Looking more closely, we can see that Hunter is an extremely complex organism. He has bones, muscles, skin, eyes, a brain, and many other components, each performing its own function. We can therefore describe a cat as a **Structure** composed of many other structures, all working together to sustain his life. These structures are not independent components simply placed next to one another. Their organization enables the organism to function as a whole.

A structure alone is not enough to describe a living system. What matters is also what happens within that structure and how it interacts with its environment. Hunter's body contains many structures, but his life unfolds through **Processes**: breathing, digesting food, moving, observing his surroundings, processing information, and responding to what happens around him. Some processes maintain his body, while others allow him to explore his environment and satisfy his needs. These processes do not exist independently. They interact, depend on one another, and contribute to the operation of the system as a whole. All processes consume energy, and some processes help the system obtain the resources it needs to continue existing. A living organism is never entirely inactive. Even when it appears to be doing nothing, processes continue to sustain its existence and maintain its internal state.

When we watch Hunter play, we can see how skillfully he moves. Every paw, every movement of his body is precisely coordinated with the others. Such coordination requires more than muscles and a brain. His body must continuously synchronize the activity of its many components. In other words, Hunter's movements depend on **Time** - internal timing mechanisms that allow his muscles to work together in a coordinated way.

There is another interesting detail: Hunter is not an isolated system. He eats a quail for breakfast, and that quail becomes part of his organism's internal processes until it is fully digested. The food is transformed, its energy and nutrients are absorbed, and its remaining matter is eventually eliminated. In this sense, Hunter is constantly exchanging matter and energy with his environment. What enters his body becomes part of the processes that sustain his existence. His life is therefore not simply a sequence of information-processing operations. It is a continuous process of maintaining and transforming a physical system.

We have now identified the fundamental elements needed to describe life from an engineering perspective:
- **Spaces** — where a living system exists and interacts with its environment.
- **Structures** — the components that make up the system, from individual organs to the organism as a whole.
- **Processes** — the activities that sustain the system and enable it to interact with its surroundings.
- **Time** — the dimension in which processes unfold and the system's components coordinate their activity.
- **OCA loop** — the cycle through which the live creature gathers information, processes it, and acts on the results.
- **Energy** — what the system needs to sustain its processes and continue to exist.

Together, these elements provide an engineering framework for describing a living system, its internal organization, its behavior, and its interaction with the world.

This framework shifts the question away from familiar biological characteristics alone. Reproduction, for example, is not a necessary condition for an individual to be alive. Nor is information processing sufficient to establish that a system is alive. What matters is how the system's organization, processes, actions, and access to energy are connected. Hunter is alive because he is not merely processing information or consuming energy. He is a physical system whose continuous activity, needs, and interactions with the world are inseparable from his continued existence. 

Also, we can use this framework to describe companies as live systems in some space of legal entities, or even to create artificial life forms inside our virtual environments.
